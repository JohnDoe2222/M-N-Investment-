V.Tax Implications of Rental Ownership

Rental profit on a non-principal-residence property is taxable income in Canada. You're taxed on net rental income — gross rent and other property income, less eligible deductible expenses — added to your other income and taxed at your combined marginal rate. The summary below is general CRA guidance, not personalized advice; we'd recommend confirming the specifics with your accountant before closing.

Deductible Current Expenses

  • Mortgage interest — the interest portion of your mortgage payments, and interest on money borrowed to improve the property (not the principal repayment portion).
  • Property taxes and landlord/liability insurance premiums, for the period the property is rented.
  • Maintenance and repairs — day-to-day costs like fixing leaks, painting between tenants, or pest control.
  • Utilities you pay on the tenants' behalf — water, electricity, gas, or internet.
  • Professional and management fees — property managers, tenant placement agents, accountants, and lawyers for lease preparation or eviction proceedings.
  • Advertising costs to find tenants.

Non-Deductible Expenses

  • Mortgage principal repayment — this builds personal equity and isn't a current expense.
  • The value of your own labour — if you do repairs yourself, only the out-of-pocket cost of materials is deductible, not a value for your time.
  • Land transfer tax — not a current expense; it's added to the capital cost of the property instead.

Capital Expenses & Capital Cost Allowance (CCA)

Major upgrades that add lasting value — a new roof, finishing the basement, building a deck — are capital expenses, not current ones, and can't be deducted in full the year they're incurred. Instead, Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) lets you deduct a percentage of the building's cost and depreciation over several years. Claiming CCA reduces today's taxable rental profit, but can trigger a "recapture" tax on sale if the asset has appreciated. Because this is an investment property rather than a principal residence, claiming CCA doesn't put a principal-residence exemption at risk — but whether deferring tax this way suits your longer-term strategy is worth a direct conversation with your accountant.

Note — General Information, Not Tax Advice Ross Mogridge and Ridge Estate are not licensed tax advisors. The summary above reflects general CRA rules for rental property ownership as of 2026 and is provided to frame the investment decision — not as a substitute for advice from your own accountant, who can confirm how these rules apply to your specific financial situation before you finalize an offer.

VI.Rural Infrastructure & Regulatory Risk

This property runs on decentralized rural infrastructure — a drilled well, a water cistern, and a septic system — rather than municipal water and sewer, which introduces cost categories an urban investor wouldn't otherwise budget for. Its location facing the Grand River adds a layer of conservation-authority oversight on top of that. None of this is disqualifying, but each item below belongs in the underwriting.

Water Supply & Septic

Bulk Water Delivery$130–$225/load depending on hauler and volume; a two-unit, five-bedroom property could consume 4,000–6,000 gallons/month if the landlord covers it — potentially $250–$400/month in erosion to NOI

Septic Pumping$350–$400 per pump-out, typically needed every 3–5 years depending on usage

Note — Lease Terms Should Assign These Costs to Tenants The pro forma in Section IV assumes $0/month in landlord-paid water costs, which only holds if every lease explicitly assigns bulk water delivery and cistern monitoring to the tenant, and if the separate hydro meters are used to put each unit's electrical account in the tenant's own name. Without those lease terms in place, water costs alone could erase $250–$400/month of the modeled cash flow.

Grand River Conservation Authority (GRCA) & Insurance

The GRCA regulates development, construction, and site alteration within river valleys and floodplains under Ontario Regulation 41/24. If any portion of the 0.34-acre lot falls within the regulated floodplain, exterior work — including a septic repair — requires a GRCA permit before Haldimand County will issue a building permit. Permit fees range from $465 for minor, low-risk work up to $10,230 for major alterations, with review timelines of 30 to 90 days. Overland flood insurance is a separate optional endorsement on top of standard landlord coverage, and given the property's riverfront exposure, it should be treated as a required line item, not an option — already reflected in the ~$200/month insurance estimate in Section IV.

Rent Control & the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB)

Because this is a pre-2018 legacy property, both units are subject to full rent control under the Residential Tenancies Act. The 2026 guideline increase is capped at 2.1% — the lowest in four years — requiring 90 days' written notice (Form N1) and limited to once every 12 months. If operating costs rise faster than 2.1% annually, NOI will compress over time unless an Above Guideline Increase application is filed with the LTB, which is a costly, evidence-heavy process. Eviction timelines for non-payment currently average 3.2–5 months from filing to first hearing; a dedicated cash reserve of four to six months' gross operating expenses is the standard prudent buffer against a delinquent tenant.

Note — A Legal Basement Third Unit Would Be Rent-Control-Exempt If the currently unfinished basement were converted into a new, code-compliant accessory dwelling unit that was never previously occupied, that specific unit would fall outside the 2018 rent-control cutoff and could be priced at market rate without the 2.1% ceiling — a longer-term value-add worth keeping in mind, separate from the base two-unit purchase decision.

Short-Term Rental as an Alternative Strategy

Haldimand County currently has no short-term rental licensing bylaw, unlike Hamilton, Mississauga, or Toronto. STR operators are still bound by standard property-standards, noise, and parking bylaws, and as of January 1, 2026, the Ontario Fire Code requires carbon monoxide alarms near all sleeping areas and on every floor of any STR, province-wide. STR income over $30,000 across four consecutive quarters requires HST registration. Given the river frontage, a hybrid strategy is worth considering: a stable long-term tenant in the larger 3-bedroom unit to cover the base mortgage, with the 2-bedroom unit run as a higher-yield short-term rental — though this isn't reflected in the base-case pro forma in Section IV, which assumes both units are leased long-term.

VII.Zoning & Legal Duplex Status

Every projection in this package — the rent roll, the pro forma, the cap rate — depends on this property being a legally recognized two-unit dwelling. That status is not yet confirmed, and it's the single largest open question in this purchase.

Flag — Legal Duplex Status Is Unverified The listing describes the home as "currently set up as a two family dwelling," which is a description of its physical layout, not a statement of legal compliance. In Ontario, a property is only a recognized legal duplex once the municipality has issued the zoning permissions and closed building permits and fire code inspections for the secondary suite. Because this property has been occupied by the same family for 38 years, there is a real chance the second unit was used informally by family members without ever going through that process — making it "legally non-conforming" at best, or simply unpermitted at worst. Zoning H A10F3 (confirmed identically across the MLS sheet, MPAC, and GeoWarehouse) does not by itself answer this question. If the second unit is found to be unpermitted, the Ontario Fire Code requires a minimum 30-to-45-minute fire-rated separation between units, interconnected smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and properly sized egress windows in every bedroom — and bringing an existing in-law suite up to the Ontario Building Code standard that took effect January 1, 2025 can run into the tens of thousands of dollars in design, permitting, and retrofit costs. This should be resolved, in writing, before conditions are waived.

How to Resolve This

  • Submit a formal Zoning Verification and Work Order request to Haldimand County confirming whether the second unit is a legal duplex, legally non-conforming, or unpermitted. Ridge Estate to initiate
  • Make the purchase offer conditional on receiving and reviewing that written confirmation before the financing and inspection conditions are waived. Built into Section VIII strategy
  • If the unit is unpermitted, obtain a contractor estimate for fire-separation and egress retrofit costs before deciding whether to proceed, renegotiate, or walk. Buyer's contractor to quote if needed

Rationale

The straight $/sq ft comp set in Section II isn't a reliable guide here — it's built from conventional single-family homes, not income duplexes on private services, and points to a value nearly double the ask. The right anchor is the income approach: at full list price, this property already prices to a 6.68% cap rate and roughly 5.3% cash-on-cash return on the conservative rent case, which is a solid starting point in a 0.9%-vacancy market. But two things argue for offering below list rather than at it: the property has already sat 24 days with one price reduction and an explicit "not holding offers" posture, and the legal duplex status flagged in Section VII carries real, unquantified cost exposure that a full-price offer doesn't account for. An opening offer of $515,000–$535,000 pushes the cap rate to roughly 6.9%–7.1% and cash-on-cash return to 6.5%–7.5%, while still being a credible, non-insulting number the seller can work with given the softer market signal.

Suggested terms: financing (five business days), a home inspection condition (seven to ten days) with specific riders for the septic system, drilled well flow rate and water quality, and cistern condition, a zoning verification condition tying directly to Section VII — satisfactory written confirmation from Haldimand County of the second unit's legal status, with a right to negotiate or walk away if it comes back unpermitted — and a vacant possession condition, which should be straightforward given both units are already unoccupied.

Note — Septic Risk as a Negotiating Point, Not a Price Reduction Rather than trying to price a possible $24,000–$65,000 septic replacement into the opening offer, we'd recommend handling it through the inspection condition itself — if the septic inspection comes back showing the system is near end-of-life, that becomes grounds to renegotiate price or request a seller credit at that point, with real inspection data behind the ask rather than a speculative discount today.
